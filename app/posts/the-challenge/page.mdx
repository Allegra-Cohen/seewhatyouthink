## Introduction

Boy, you think you know where you're going when you sign off on a post! You don't. Things can always get more complicated. 

Last time I defined a north star: Tools for thought that don't degrade our cognitive abilities. I also said I would identify the bottlenecks that might be preventing progress towards these tools. But the thing is, making any kind of significant progress towards *your* north star requires other people. Which means you don't just need to understand what the path to your star looks like -- you need to understand what other people think, too, especially if their own north stars have particular gravity that will hasten or warp your path. And even if you find allies whose stars align with yours,<Footnote>Aw, cute ;)</Footnote> you might have very different ideas of the best way to reach them.

I suspect that figuring out this constellation of stars and paths is part of what people call "field-mapping." In that spirit, this post is more of a field report. I do, in fact, have one or two suggestions for levers that could catalyze innovation around poietic tools. But I also think the process I went through to design those suggestions might be interesting to others. So instead of just saying what my recommendations are, I'm going to start with how I got there.<Footnote>You might wonder if that wasn't the point of the first two parts. Here's a picture I've been thinking about a lot:

The y-axis is how good an LLM (or, let's say, AI capabilities in general) is at a thing. The x-axis is how good we are at a thing. The dots are things. The dotted line is the line of parity, where AI and humans are equally good at a task. We can expect that as AI gets better, some things will move across the line towards the AI side. But that could happen for two reasons:
1. AI gets better.
2. We get worse.

Note that these outcomes aren't independent across things and they're not symmetric, either. As we saw in the first part of this series, AI getting better at one thing could potentially degrade our capabilities for others. And us getting worse at something (e.g., navigating conflict through discussion) doesn't mean AI will pick up the slack. If we get worse and AI does not get better, we could "lose" things entirely. 

At the same time, whether and why a thing crosses the line doesn't always have a universal moral color, whether or not it is lost. Take memorizing epic poems as an example: Socrates thought writing would degrade our memories; most of us today don't care that we can't recite more than the first two lines of the good part of St Crispin's Day speech.<Footnote>WE FEW WE HAPPY FEW WE BA</Footnote> Now take letter-writing as an example. ## The Thing

We're going to focus on a well-studied and pretty important thing: Sensemaking.

"Sensemaking" is a term used by Pirolli and Card to describe how intelligence analysts turn heaps of disconnected data into clear narratives.<Reference id= "pirolli_2005_sensemaking"/> Take a look at their picture of what intelligence analysis looks like as a whole:

![](/drawings/pirolli.png)

You have lower-order tasks like searching for information, filtering it, skimming it, and so on. Pirolli and Card term this the "foraging loop." Then you have higher-order tasks like schema building, hypothesis testing and storytelling. These parts are what Pirolli and Card call "sensemaking." Right now, LLMs live mostly in the bottom left part of the picture, and they're not even great at that.<Reference/> Humans are good at the top right part of the picture, with two caveats: 1) We need to go through the whole process to get good at the top bits, and 2) It can take us a very long time to get the right answers.<Footnote>Think back to my friend's story about the CIA analyst who caught bin Laden. She did it, but it took something like ten months.</Footnote>

Ok, so sensemaking is our chosen thing. Take as given that we want to get better at it.<Footnote>That is because we want to catch terrorists faster, not to mention that we also do sensemaking when we're catching money launderers, tracing a pandemic, investing money, donating money, mapping a field, doing a Google search, talking to our therapist, etc.</Footnote> Take also as a given that there is a *lot* of research on how humans learn and think, a *lot* of research on how humans do sensemaking specifically, a *lot* of research on how humans share mental models in teams, and even a *lot* of research on how technology can help us do these things faster.<Footnote>Much of it funded by DARPA, much of it bottlenecked by AI. For example, DARPA's Causal Exploration program. When I was at DARPA, Dan Adams took me through the UI for collectively building and visualizing causal models. It was great, he said, but the whole thing hinged on an ontology that had to be manually updated.</Footnote> But LLMs are so new, so much better than past NLP, and sometimes still so much worse than humans that how to integrate them into sensemaking isn't clear. And that's before we start worrying about possible deleterious effects on human ability. 

## The Lever

Let me pause for a moment to tell you a story you have probably already heard. In 2004, the DoD wanted autonomous vehicles to be a thing, and the research world was moving too slowly. So DARPA offered $1 million to any team with a self-driving car that could drive 150 miles through the Mojave Desert. The DARPA Grand Challenge, as it was known, was very popular, super difficult and heralded as the beginning of the self-driving car industry. It sparked progress and innovation through the <a href="https://www.renaissancephilanthropy.org/playbooks/market-shaping">definition and incentivizing of a clear goal.</a> 

Challenges are useful when there is a) a clear objective and we b) don't know the best way to get to it. This is true of sensemaking: We have a bunch of ideas about how machines can help do it, but none has been proven the best and many of the most innovative approaches are still prototypes. So that's one box checked on the list of criteria for a challenge. Another thing about sensemaking: The success condition is clear. No matter how fuzzy and mysterious and remarkable the stuff inside our heads is, at the end of the day, we've either caught bin Laden faster or we haven't.<Footnote>Or, you know, caught more bin Ladens, or what have you. We'll discuss the details later.</Footnote> Clear objective, many possible paths, and simple rules for winning: These, my friends, are good conditions for a challenge.

But hold on, you might be thinking -- you didn't actually state an objective. Do we want to get better at sensemaking, or do we want to get better at sensemaking in a way that doesn't damage our own skills and expertise? We can't avoid answering this, because it changes the design of the challenge. And, hey, you and I both know what I think.

## Design

Disclaimer: I'm not married to a lot of the details below. I've already had lovely conversations with folks that suggest the best design probably hasn't been written yet. But here's one early attempt.

Let's say for simplicity's sake that you and I work at the Challenge House ("the House" for short) and we're in charge. We issue the following challenge to communities across human-computer interaction, AI, NLP, modeling, operational psychology, cognitive science, augmented reality, hardware, and any other field that wants to get involved: **Design tools and environments to solve complex sensemaking problems.**

We say that the challenge will occur one year after its announcement. Honestly, in this economy, we probably provide seed funding to some teams -- let's say a pot of $10 million with a maximum award of $500k. The solicitation says, "However you want to do it, build something that solves the problem."

In the meantime, the House team gets busy. We hire a testing and evaluation (T&E) team and kick the really thorny design and statistical problems over to them. These are things like, please design a sensemaking challenge that cannot be solved by an out-of-the-box LLM, does not have floor or ceiling effects for human sensemakers, and offers enough opportunity for interesting posthoc analysis about the competing technologies.<Footnote>If you're skeptical about whether problem sets like this exist, I was too -- but it turns out they do! There are a bunch of sensemaking datasets used quite frequently in training and competitions. "Sign of the Crescent" is a classic, where you have to dodge red herrings and sleuth through lots of data to discover a terrorist plot. Of course, the solution to that dataset is on the internet, so we probably need to build our own.</Footnote> This team will have smarter ideas than me, but for the purpose of this post, assume that they design a problem set where the winner is the one that solves the problem fastest.

We also make friends with the intelligence and law enforcement communities, maybe roping a few analyst training programs into supplying us with human *sensemakers*. These are the people who will use the technology built by the competitors in the challenge. We won't give teams' technologies to novices because then we have to account for learning the task; we're giving teams' technologies to people who are already expert in the task.<Footnote>Well, maybe? Unless we think it's actually useful to have prior knowledge start at zero. IDK man, there's a lot to figure out.</Footnote>

The day of the challenge arrives! Competing teams and sensemakers congregate in a location.<Footnote>Maybe somewhere with a beach? And a good spot for birding?</Footnote> Each team is assigned a set of sensemakers. When the challenge begins, these sensemakers use their team's technology to solve the problem set by the T&E team (individually, that is. Each sensemaker works with the technology in isolation.) Because we're interested in the effects of the tool, we're also going to have a control group working old school.<Footnote>Let's define this as a computer with a monitor, a search engine with AI overview turned off, and various notetaking and drawing tools commonly available on today's computers. Maybe they get a printer, too.</Footnote> Teams are scored by looking at the difference in time to solve between their sensemakers and the control group.

By the end of the day, the data look something like this<Footnote>Please note that I have no idea what the results would look like, that's the whole point of running a challenge</Footnote> (where the score is how much *faster* the team was than the control group):

This tells us that one particular approach did much better than the others: Different modalities (haptics and visual data environments) shared by humans and LLMs. If our T&E team is really good, we probably have a lot of other data points for posthoc analyses. For example, perhaps approaches with the most human direction got to certain steps in the solution much faster than approaches that leaned more heavily on AI. Maybe the team that went hard on neurosymbolic strategies did particularly well at tasks that required holding many pieces of information in mind at once...and so on. 

At the end of the day, the winner is crowned. The T&E team bends towards the work of analyzing the results, publishing a report some amount of time later. ## Impact
I like the challenge idea because, if done well, it's both very flashy and pretty darn informative. For example, if some teams come out miles ahead, then we learn what approaches funders and investors should fund and invest in. Similarly, if teams crash and burn, we get a signal about what approaches don't work. Even if the race is tight, provided that the T&E team did their jobs right, we can see where different approaches pulled ahead or fell behind. At the end of the day, researchers, funders and investors should all learn a lot about promising ways to bring AI into sensemaking.

What about the challenge's impact beyond sensemaking? Or rather, sensemaking-the-process beyond the intelligence context? Lots of jobs entail sensemaking besides intelligence analysis and criminal investigation. Epidemiological tracing, for one. All kinds of investing, for another. Field-mapping for philanthropic foundations. Policy design (ideally.) Even your Google search (or Claude prompt) is a kind of sensemaking. It's not beyond belief to imagine the winner of this challenge could go on to build a very lucrative piece of fintech, design the new search experience or (why not!) detect the next pandemic.

Another impact this challenge might have is on neurosymbolic AI research, and particularly its transition out of the lab. Recall that the T&E team is tasked with designing a challenge problem that LLMs can't solve alone out of the box. That means that teams have to think about the structure of sensemaking and figure out how to bring LLMs into it. There are some very innovative projects being done right now around how to share structure with LLMs that go far beyond RAG. This challenge might incentivize more aggressive progress in that area.

To the last point, you'll note that nothing about the challenge indicates that you can't try to solve it with AI alone. I thought about whether there should be an explicit condition for teams that build "AI alone" approaches (i.e., the system solves the problem and simply hands it to the human sensemaker to submit.) But then I thought, people are probably going to try to do that anyways. Why would I draw artificial divisions? Let people take bets -- both practical and normative -- on whether they think humans should be in the loop.

Speaking of which, this is not the challenge design I would pick! It might point me roughly north, but it does not get me as close to my north star as I would like. <p style={{ textAlign: "center" }}>Magic Hands total score = &Delta;(&Delta;(Control Day 1, Control Day 2), &Delta;(Magic Hands Day 1, Magic Hands Day 2)).</p>

Put in English, we're trying to separate the variance in outcome due to learning from the variance due to the approaches. For example, if the Magic Hands sensemakers move much faster the second day than Old School Rules, that could happen either because the Magic Hands approach is better or because the Magic Hands sensemakers didn't really use the technology but learned about the world on their own. Looking at the difference between Days 1 and 2 for the control group tells us what the outcome would be if the latter were true. Therefore, looking at the difference in the differences between days between groups (I'm sorry) lets us say how much variance is due to the approach.

The challenge I describe in the rest of this post is a very precise lever. We're saying that we know what our objective is and all we need is to fund smart people to figure out how to get there. And maybe that's true for sensemaking, but what about all of the other points on the picture I drew? Maybe some things, like writing persuasively, will cross the line because our education systems can't figure out how to keep teaching them. Maybe others, like triage, will cross the line because, whether we think it is ethical or not, machines are simply better. If we don't know what might happen for any given thing, how can we know what we want to save until it's too late? Given that, why would I focus on One Thing??

Yeah, I mean, I'm with you. I think a lot of people are. There are organizations popping up all over the place to study the economic and political impacts of AI,<Footnote>Shout out to <a href = "https://ai.objectives.institute">AOI</a></Footnote> plus the economic and political levers one could pull to "save" things one wants to save. I have noticed, however, a little gap -- maybe just for poietic tools and knowledge work, but I think it might snake its way through a lot of other domains, like medicine and education.

Here's the gap: The people who could build, raise money for and incentivize poietic tools are probably not the people who know how to design them. There's just *so* much research out there pertaining to our brains, our tools, and our brains plus our tools. To write my last two posts, I read literature from ten different and/or overlapping fields, including psychology, neuroscience, cognitive science, education, education *technology*, mixed and augmented reality, optical engineering, philosophy, economics and human-computer interaction. And a tsunami of new research on LLMs and our brains is about to come crashing down. To say nothing about the potential solution space -- there are so many demos of novel approaches that I haven't even had time to mention, like <a href = "https://notes.andymatuschak.org/z5E5QawiXCMbtNtupvxeoEX">evergreen notes</a>, <a href="https://wattenberger.com/thoughts/fish-eye/">fisheye lenses</a> and <a href="http://dynamicland.org">DynamicLand</a>. And can we pause for a minute to think about how indie art games have cracked the code on making us feel joy and serenity in digital spaces? 

I don't think it's realistic to expect the people who *can* build and popularize tools to keep up on the literature about *how* those tools should behave. And if it's not easy to build the better thing, people will default to maximizing engagement through the eager oracle. That puts downstream economic and policy levers out of work -- without alternatives to the oracular paradigm, there's nothing to incentivize. We need to bridge the gap between research and outcome faster than ever before.<Footnote>Another outcome is people doing the wrong things because those things seemed right but then the research evolved. As an example, pick, like, anything in public health today. Or think about the <a href = "https://www.sciencenews.org/article/balanced-literacy-phonics-teaching-reading-evidence">reading wars.</a></Footnote>

Here is a small and sketchy proposal for a lever to fill this gap: **the Snowglobe.**

The Snowglobe is a group of people who take the blizzard of information about what we "should" be doing and convert it into public goods that designers, programmers, engineers, entrepreneurs and even policymakers can use. The Snowglobe amasses and curates as much information as possible about:

1. What we value
2. How our brains work
3. How we can build tools to help us do what we value better

Then, they turn this information into public goods. I'm not sure exactly what that looks like right now, but for poietic tools my thought is something like a design guidebook. Some examples of existing design guidebooks are Google's <a href="https://pair.withgoogle.com/guidebook/">People + AI Research guidebook</a> and Microsoft's <a href="https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/haxtoolkit/ai-guidelines/">Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction</a>. Google's in particular does a beautiful job of laying out the principles and patterns you should use in AI product development, down to the worksheets and case studies you can use to employ and understand them.

The Snowglobe would build a guidebook for poietic tools specifically. It would be part automated literature review, part expert curation, and it would be given focus by unprecedented mountains of information about knowledge workers' priorities across the globe. You could imagine a startup entrepreneur cracking open the guidebook to decide, "Is the new AI feature all my competitors are adding worth it?" and receiving an answer based on how that feature might affect users' memories, creativity, and progress towards goals. You could even imagine this answer coming with a carbon tally -- how many tokens will it take and is the outcome worth the cost? (Now we're getting into the sketchiest parts. To do this right you need a clear objective, just like the challenge; I'm still noodling.)

The Snowglobe would not build guidebooks by hand, of course. This is why I call it a "snowglobe" instead of something like an "observatory." Observatories are for observing things that are far away; the members of the Snowglobe are moving through the same blizzard we are. In a perfect world, the Snowglobe not only produces public goods but tests and builds innovative tools for doing so faster, from <a href = "https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.21136">adaptive interviewers</a> to <a href = "https://elicit.com">AI-enabled literature reviews</a> to novel ways of visualizing field-mapping.<Footnote>Look, all I want is an e-ink tapestry that I can tack to my wall that represents knowledge work as a campaign map. And then a portable version I can roll up like a scroll and take to my meetings so it can talk to other scrolls and tell me what the rest of the world looks like. Is that so hard?</Footnote> 

Of course, building central and current curations of huge, complicated knowledge is not quite enough, because we don't always read things ourselves. It should be machine-readable, too. Easy enough, in the age of LLMs -- but because we're focusing often on *digital* tools, I think we can go a step farther and try to link software specs and open-source packages to the guidebook through a public API. For example, suppose I want to turn my office into an immersive digital workspace. I should be able to bookmark a section of the guidebook, send it to Claude to read, and then have Claude query that section and pull any packages that might help, like visualization packages and hand tracking.<Footnote>Claude can actually already sort of do this last part, if you know what to ask for. I just want something more organized.</Footnote> It should also come back with rules of thumb -- like, when I add something to the workspace, a good assistant occasionally reminds me of that thing to help me bring it into my knowledge schema.

You could imagine the Snowglobe putting out many guidebooks, from building poietic tools to evaluating them, from sensemaking and knowledge work to more focused books for education and medicine. I don't think the Snowglobe would be permanent -- the goal would be to wrangle as much information about our changing world into useful formats as possible, then wind down gracefully with just enough budget for the machines to keep the guidebooks current. But I do think that even a year or two of concerted effort could have an impact on the picture I showed at the top of this post. 

That's all I have to say on the Snowglobe! Let me know your thoughts. Or, if you suspect my proposals might be more compelling at a lower level of abstraction, check out the challenge I describe in the other paths.
